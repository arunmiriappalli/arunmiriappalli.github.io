title: "Two Ways Teams Decide What's Next"
date: 2026-09-07
description: Two teams can plan the exact same way and land on opposite instincts — and AI is making the quarterly ritual nearly free just as shipping cycles get too fast for a quarter to hold.
tags:

Every org I've worked in sits somewhere on one planning spectrum, and most people know which end they're on before they can explain why.

**One end runs on conversation.** At a startup I was at - everyone in one office - a regulator changed the rules midweek. We parked the in-flight work, someone walked the room through the new constraint at standup, and by the time we broke we were pointed elsewhere. No doc to amend, no meeting to re-open a plan, because there wasn't a formal plan to re-open. Priority lived in the room, and the room heard the news together.

What that end does well is respond. New information in, new direction out, same morning - and in payments, where a rule change or an outage can eat your week, that's worth a lot. The shadow is visibility. Ask a new hire what the roadmap is six months out and you get a shrug, because it isn't written down; it's in a few senior heads. The important-but-not-urgent work - the migration, the reconciliation cleanup - never becomes this week's fire, so it just doesn't get done.

**The other end runs on the quarter.** At the mid-size company, planning is a scheduled event: objectives, key results, confidence scores, cascading company to org to team. Distributed across time zones, that document is how people who never share a room stay aligned.

We committed to launching country A, then country B - both needing local payment methods built from scratch, both written up as key results. Then A launched soft, traction well under plan. The honest move was to pause and rethink B. We kept building B - not from conviction, but because pausing mid-quarter and re-scoping across a remote org was more friction than continuing.

That's the double edge. The same commitment that keeps a team from thrashing every Tuesday - the one that also protects the unglamorous work, the migrations and on-call cleanup that never win on their own - is the commitment that kept us building toward B after the numbers went quiet. The plan did what a plan does: it held, past the point its assumptions did.

Then there's AI. The heaviest cost of the quarterly end was always the paperwork - the planning week, the status rollup from ten teams. Most of that is near-free now: draft the structure, summarize every KR, generate the mid-quarter writeup in minutes. Which is clarifying, because once the ceremony is cheap you can see what it bought - alignment, or a document. And faster cycles are stretching the quarter thin: when a six-week feature ships in one, a twelve-week plan is betting on a stale world. The country-B problem, made structural.

So the spectrum is getting squeezed from the middle. Cheap rollups give rolling teams OKR-level visibility with no planning week; short cycles push quarterly teams to re-plan far more than four times a year.

Which end you gravitate to usually isn't about which is right. It's about which mess you'd rather own: the plan that was never written down, or the plan that outlived its assumptions.
